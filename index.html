Ahiyaniwa

Meaning "language". A modern Taíno revival rooted in authenticity and history, made for Taíno communities.

Overview

A modern Taíno language

Maburika (Welcome)! Ahiyaniwa is a modern revitalization of the Taíno language of the Greater Antilles. My name is Casey Alberto Roche, and I'm a Cuban American linguist with distant Taíno ancestry. I'm also a member of the Higuayagua Taino Tribe. My project is still young, but it grows a little more every day, and you are more than welcome to explore it!

The name

Ahiyaniwa means “language.” It is built from ahiya “to speak” plus the nominalizing material niwa, which keeps the name centered on speech, community, and everyday use.

Arawakan roots

Classic Taíno is a Ta-Arawakan language, and Ahiyaniwa seeks to maintain this core. As such, it follows Arawakan patterns: pronominal prefixes, agglutinative verbs, postpositions, stative predication, and an open syllable aesthetic.

Authenticity first

There are a few Taíno reconstructions in circulation, but Ahiyaniwa is different. It strives to keep its Arawakan authenticity and reduce English/Spanish influences by working from colonial sources, historical linguistics, and close sister languages such as Lokono and Wayuu.

Where the project stands

Ahiyaniwa is in its early stages. The dictionary is already substantial, the grammar is being standardized, and the lexicon keeps expanding. It is a work in progress, and it is worked on every day.

Orthography standard

This site uses a consistent modern spelling: accent marks on verb suffixes, tildes for nasal vowels, and an apostrophe between a prefix and a vowel initial root.

Verb suffixes: ká, bá, bó, há, té Nasal vowels: ã, ẽ, ĩ, õ, ũ Elision: d'ahiya, b'ata, w'okona
Mission

Reconnecting a Caribbean language

For a long time, conventional wisdom said Taíno was completely gone, leaving behind only loanwords such as hamaka “hammock,” hurakã “hurricane,” kanowa “canoe,” and tabako “tobacco.” Ahiyaniwa refuses to let the language stay a footnote in history.

Why this project exists

As a Taíno descendant, member of the Higuayagua Taino Tribe, and linguist, this work is deeply personal. I am dedicated to embracing and promoting Taíno language and culture. Work continues every day: refining the lexicon, testing grammatical patterns, standardizing spelling, and expanding the available materials.

Arawakan authenticity

A few Taíno reconstructions exist, but Ahiyaniwa is built to stay close to Arawakan structure. It does not invent forms out of thin air. It follows the linguistic breadcrumbs left by sixteenth century sources, historical linguists, and sister languages.

The goal is not merely to make “Taíno sounding” words. The goal is a usable language with authentic Arawakan morphology, syntax, and phonological shape that learners can acquire quickly.

From colonial sources

Chroniclers such as Fray Ramón Pané, Bartolomé de las Casas, and Gonzalo Fernández de Oviedo recorded Taíno words, place names, personal names, and short phrases. But they were not trained phoneticians. They wrote with sixteenth century Spanish spelling, which is why historical spellings like Buren, Caiman, and Tiburon have to be filtered into forms like burẽ, kaimã, and tiburõ.

From sister languages

When the historical record runs out, Ahiyaniwa compares Taíno with Lokono, Wayuu, and reconstructed Proto-Lokono-Wayuu roots. Regular sound correspondences help bridge lexical gaps while respecting the open syllable rhythm, intervocalic lenition, and Arawakan grammatical skeleton of Taíno.

Mission statement: Ahiyaniwa is a labor of love. It is an always expanding, community-centered reconstruction that honors the ancestors through Arawakan structure, Caribbean sound, and usability in the modern world.

Syllable structure

CV, V, and CVC are all possible. Any consonant can start a syllable, but the coda is highly restricted and consonant clusters are avoided. The language strongly favors open syllables and a smooth, vowel final rhythm.

Template: CV, V, CVC.

Phonological rules

Allophony: /d/ becomes [ɾ] between vowels. In Taíno writing, d occurs word initially, while r is used between vowels.

Stress: usually penultimate, but final if the word ends in /i/, the diphthong /ei/, or a nasal vowel. Vowel suffixes also make stress word final.

Grammar

Arawakan grammar, clearly organized

Ahiyaniwa is agglutinative and synthetic, which means it likes to pack a lot of meaning into single words. It uses bound morphemes for person, possession, tense, and aspect, and it prefers verb first word order. Do not worry if it looks like a lot at first. Once you see the patterns, it is remarkably regular. Let us walk through it together, one piece at a time.

1. Pronominal prefixes

These little prefixes are the building blocks of the language. Attach one to a noun and it shows possession (“my,” “your,” “his”). Attach one to a verb and it shows who is doing the action (“I,” “you,” “he”).

PersonSingularPlural
1stda (my / I)wa (our / we)
2ndbu (your / you)hu (your PL / you PL)
3rd Masc.li (his / he)na (their / they)
3rd Fem.tu (her / she)na (their / they)
3rd Inan.to (its / it)na (their / they)

Noun example: d'ako (da + ako) “my eye.” Verb example: daká (da + ká) “I am.”

2. Independent emphatic pronouns

Because the prefix on the verb already tells you who is doing the action, you do not strictly need a separate subject word. But when you want to emphasize the actor (“I did it myself”), answer a question with a standalone pronoun, or make a contrast, add the focus element kiya to the pronominal root.

dakiya “I / me / myself,” bukiya “you / yourself,” likiya “he / himself,” tukiya “she / herself,” wakiya “we / ourselves,” hukiya “you all / yourselves,” nakiya “they / themselves.” The inanimate pronoun remains simply to.

When used in a sentence, these usually go at the very front for emphasis, right before the prefixed verb: wakiya w'ahiyaba “we ourselves spoke.”

3. Object pronouns

When a pronoun is on the receiving end of an action (me, you, him, her, us, them), or attached to a directional ending like “to me” or “with us,” Ahiyaniwa uses special object forms. They are placed after the verb or attached directly to a postposition.

de “me,” wo “us,” bu “you,” hu “you all,” li “him,” tu “her,” to “it,” nu “them.”

Examples: bumũ “to you,” demũ “for me,” numũ “to them.”

4. The vowel drop rule (elision)

Taíno loves smooth, open syllables, so it avoids awkward vowel collisions at prefix boundaries. Whenever a prefix ending in a vowel meets a root that starts with a vowel, drop the prefix vowel. This is often marked with an apostrophe in writing.

da (my) + anixi (heart) becomes d'anixi “my heart.”
wa (we) + okona (walk) + ba (past) becomes w'okonaba “we walked.”

Elision is entirely regular. It applies wherever the prefix meets a vowel initial root, including before postpositions.

5. Articles and determiners

Ahiyaniwa uses both bound prefixes and free particles to mark definiteness.

ka is an attributive definite prefix for inanimate nouns (kabohiyo “the house”). li is a free article for masculine human nouns (li kasike “the chief”). to is the free article for feminine and inanimate nouns (to hamaka “the hammock”). Indefinite aba means “a” or “an,” and ababe or abano mean “some” or “several.”

6. Pluralization

Plural suffixes are common but not obligatory when the noun is already modified by a number word or a plural determiner.

no is used for animate and human nouns: kasikeno “chiefs,” taino “good people.” be is used for inanimate and general nouns: arabe “trees,” anabe “flowers.”

You can say biyã ara “two trees” without adding be, since the numeral already marks the plural.

7. Postpositions

Unlike English, which puts prepositions before the noun, Ahiyaniwa puts its relation words after the noun. They are called postpositions, and they attach to the end of the noun or pronoun. Like prefixes, when attached to words ending in a vowel, they follow the vowel-drop (elision) rule. When a postposition that starts with a vowel follows a nasal vowel, an epenthetic consonant is inserted.

mũ “to / for,” oriya “from / of,” oma “with / in the company of,” abo “using / with,” yo “toward,” kowa “at / on,” oloko “inside.”

Examples: arabukoyo “toward the forest,” d'awõmabo “with my dog,” kabohiyo'loko “inside the house.”

8. Verb morphology and tense

Every active verb follows the same simple formula: pronoun prefix + verb root + tense or aspect suffix. The main suffixes are:

ká present, stative, or habitual, bá past or perfective, bó progressive, há future or irrealis, and té imperative.

With ahiya “to speak”: b'ahiyabá “you spoke,” d'ahiyabó “I am speaking,” tuboriká “she works,” d'ahiyahá “I will speak.”

9. The copula “to be”

Ahiyaniwa does not have a separate verb root for “to be.” Instead, the stative suffix ká attaches directly to the pronoun root: daká “I am,” buká “you are,” liká “he is,” tuká “she is,” toká “it is,” waká “we are,” huká “you all are,” naká “they are.”

To shift the tense, just swap the suffix: past dabá “I was,” future dahá “I will be,” progressive dabó “I am being.”

10. Negation

To say that something is not happening or not in a certain state, attach the negative prefix ma (“without” or “non occurrence”) to the front of the verb or stative root.

maxaliká “is not happy,” matoxaliká “it is not happy,” mad'arikẽká d'itu “I do not see my sister.”

The negative prefix behaves just like any other prefix. It simply goes at the very front of the word.

11. Imperatives (commands)

Commands are formed by prefixing the person you are addressing and adding té to the verb root: b'ahiyaté! “Speak!” hukãmaté! “You all listen!”

One common exception: “let us go” is usually just w'aibá! rather than w'aibate!

12. Word order and sentence building

Ahiyaniwa prefers verb first order: V S O or V S Location. Even when a full noun subject appears, the verb at the beginning keeps its matching pronoun prefix.

Toxaliká d'anixi kawayabakowa “My heart is happy at the guava tree,” literally “It is happy, my heart, at the guava tree.”

Modifiers generally come before the noun they describe: semiku wayaba “sweet guava.”

13. Demonstratives

“This” and “that” agree with the noun's gender class. For masculine human nouns: lihi and lihino “this” and “these,” lira and lirano “that” and “those.” For feminine human nouns: tuhi and tuhino, tura and turano. For inanimates: toho and tohobe, tora and torabe.

Example: toho bohiyo “this house,” torabe arabuko “those forests.”

14. Comparatives and numbers

The comparative suffix ha means “er”: tuyuha “taller.” The standalone word sabo “more” can also be used, often with the ablative oriya: liká li kasike sabo tuyu li ir'oriya “the chief is taller than the man.”

The numbers one through ten are: abã, biyã, kanokũ, bibiti, abaka, abati, karaxi, kamiti, bitiya, abãraka.

Deep dive

Two ways to say “I am”

One of the nicest things about Ahiyaniwa is how flexible its stative clauses are. You get two equally valid paths for linking a subject to an adjective, and you can choose whichever one fits the moment.

The synthetic path, compact and traditional

Sandwich the adjective between the pronoun prefix and the stative suffix ká. This produces a single, tightly woven word and gives you that classic Arawakan rhythm.

Daxaliká “I am happy.” Busiwatuká “You are tired.” L'anuyuká “He is sick.” L'ahiyatiká teketa “He is very talkative.”

The analytic path, clear and easy to process

Use the standalone copula daká, buká, toká and then place the unmodified adjective right after it. This makes it easy to drop in modifiers like teketa “very.”

Daká xali “I am happy.” Buká hamuwei “You are hungry.” Waká tata “We are strong.” Toká teketa anako “It is very beautiful.”

Both are completely grammatical. Many speakers reach for the analytic form when they are building fluency or emphasizing a modifier, and the synthetic form in poetry, storytelling, or whenever they want that flowing Arawakan feel. Try mixing both into your practice and see which one fits your voice.

Suffixes

Noun and verb suffixes

Ahiyaniwa builds most of its vocabulary by adding meaningful pieces to a root. Some suffixes attach to nouns, others attach to verbs, and a few work with both. Here is a tour of the most useful ones, each with at least one example so you can see how it behaves in a real word.

Noun suffixes

no animate / human plural
Marks more than one person, animal, or living thing.

Attach it to a human or animate noun to make it plural. You can skip it when a number word already shows the plural.

kasike “chief” → kasikeno “chiefs”
inaru “woman” → inaruno “women”
be inanimate / general plural
Marks more than one object, place, or general noun.

Use it on inanimate and general nouns. It is the everyday plural for things rather than people.

ara “tree” → arabe “trees”
ana “flower” → anabe “flowers”
ti / tu agent marker
Turns a verb into the person who does the action. ti is masculine, tu is feminine.

Attach it to a verb root to name the doer. It is one of the most productive suffixes in the language.

ahiya “speak” → ahiyati “male speaker,” ahiyatu “female speaker”
bori “work” → boriti “male worker”
ro / li nominalizer
Makes a noun out of a root. ro is feminine and li is masculine.

Use it when the noun refers to a female person or a feminine thing.

irahu “young” → irahuro “girl”
irahu “young” → irahuli “boy”
to nominalizer
Turns a verb or adjective into a noun for the action or the thing itself.

It is a general purpose nominalizer and works well for names of events, objects, or results.

arei “sing” → areito “ceremonial song and dance”
bori “work” → borito “job”
ya essence / instrumental
Names the inner essence of something, or the tool used to do it.

It often appears on nouns that name a quality or a thing that embodies that quality.

kabu “tie” → kabuya “rope”
bori “work” → boriya “peasant/commoner
hu abstract nominalizer
Turns a verb into the idea or abstract concept behind the action.

Use it when you want to talk about an action as a thing, like “speech” from “speak.”

ahiya “speak” → ahiyahu “speech, the act of speaking”
eixu “think” → eixuhu “thought”
wa adjectival
Makes an adjective meaning “having the quality of” or “relating to” the root.

Attach it to a noun when you want to describe something as being full of, made of, or like that noun.

wehi “sun” → wehiwa “sunny”
turei “sky” → tureiwa “heavenly, celestial”
el patronymic
Means “son of” or “descendant of,” used in names and kin terms.

It appears mostly in personal names and lineage words. It is one of the rare suffixes that can end a word in a consonant.

semi “spirit” → semi(y)el “son of the spirit”
waroko “our grandfather” → warokoel “descendant of our grandfather”
ha comparative
Makes an adjective comparative, like “er” in English.

Attach it to an adjective to say that something has more of that quality than something else.

tuyu “tall” → tuyuha “taller”
xoko “small” → xokoha “smaller”

Verb suffixes

ká present / stative / habitual
Marks an ongoing state or something done regularly. Also serves as the copula “am, is, are.”

This is the most common verb suffix. It covers present tense, habitual actions, and states of being.

d'ahiyaká “I speak”
tuboriká “she works”
daká “I am”
bá past / perfective
Marks a finished action in the past.

Use it when the action is complete, whether it happened recently or long ago.

b'ahiyabá “you spoke”
n'arikēbá “they saw”
dabá “I was”
bó progressive
Marks an action happening right now, like “ing” in English.

It is the suffix you reach for when you want to say that something is actively in progress.

d'ahiyabó “I am speaking”
d'abokabó “I am cooking”
w'areibó “we are singing”
há future / irrealis
Marks something that will happen, might happen, or is intended.

Use it for future plans and hypothetical situations alike.

d'ahiyahá “I will speak”
w'okonahá “we will travel”
dahá “I will be”
té imperative
Makes a command or a request.

Attach it to the verb root after the subject prefix to tell someone to do something.

b'ahiyaté! “Speak!”
hukãmaté! “You all listen!”
b'ataté! “Drink!”
ra verbalizer
Turns a noun or adjective into a verb meaning “to do” or “to make” that thing.

It is the standard way to create a new verb from a noun. The resulting verb then takes normal tense suffixes.

axula “kiss” → axulara “to kiss”
kusiba “ice” → kusibara “to freeze, to ice”
a become
A prefix that means “to become” the quality named by the root.

Strictly speaking this one goes at the front of the word, but it behaves like a derivational suffix in how it changes the meaning of a root.

sanako “stupid” → asanako “to become stupid”
xali “happy” → axali “to become happy”
ma negative prefix
Means “without” or “not,” and negates the word it attaches to.

Like the “become” prefix, it goes at the front of the word rather than the end, but it belongs in this list because it is one of the most important meaning changing pieces in the language.

xali “happy” → maxali “unhappy”
kabu “importance” → makabu “unimportant”
suruti “right, correct” → masuruti “wrong”

Example Postpositions

kowa locative postposition
Means “at,” “on,” or “at the edge of.”

Attach it to a place noun to say where something happens. It also appears in compound place names.

burẽ “griddle” → kaburẽkowa “at the griddle”
beyabu “beach” → kabeyabukowa “at the beach”
mũ dative postposition
Means “to” or “for.”

Use it to mark the person or thing that receives the action or benefits from it.

de “me” → demũ “to me, for me”
bohiyo “house” → kabohiyomũ “to the house”
oriya ablative / genitive postposition
Means “from,” “out of,” or “of.”

Use it to mark the source of something or the material it is made from.

siba “stone” → sib'oriya “of stone”
hiwera “gourd” → kahiwer'oriya “from the gourd”
oma associative postposition
Means “with” in the sense of “in the company of.”

Use it when you are together with a person or animal, rather than using a tool.

wo “us” → w'oma “with us”
awō “dog” → d'awõmoma “with my dog”
abo instrumental postposition
Means “with” in the sense of “using” or “by means of.”

Use it when the noun is the tool or material used to do something. It often needs an epenthetic m or n before it.

kawõna “gold” → kawõn'abo “with gold, gold containing”
yo directional postposition
Means “toward” or “in the direction of.”

Attach it to a place noun to show motion in that direction.

arabuko “forest” → karabukoyo “toward the forest”
ikẽ “land” → kikẽyo “toward the land”
oloko inessive postposition
Means “inside” or “within.”

Use it to say that something is contained within a place or object.

bohiyo “house” → kabohiy'oloko “inside the house”
arabuko “forest” → karabuk'oloko “inside the forest”
kei similative postposition
Means “like” or “as.”

Use it to compare one thing to another without saying they are the same.

semi “spirit” → semikei “like a spirit”
awara “bird” → awarakei “like a bird”
A note on building words. You can stack suffixes in a single word as long as the meaning stays clear. A form like ahiya “speak” can grow into ahiyati “speaker,” then ahiyatihu “the quality of being a speaker,” and so on. The language rewards patience and pattern spotting, and the more you read, the more natural the combinations will feel.

Greetings and introductions

Miyari kena maburika! Toká d'iri Maria, tuwei toká b'iri?
Hello and welcome! My name is Maria, what is your name?
Miyari “hello,” kena “and,” maburika “welcome.” Toká “it is” plus d'iri “my name.” Tuwei “what,” toká “it is,” b'iri “your name.”
Taiwei! Halika buká ukairã?
Good morning! How are you today?
Taiwei “good morning,” halika “how,” buká “you are,” ukairã “today.”
Daká teketa xali, hahõ! Kena d'ahiyabó ahiyaniwa taino b'oma.
I am very happy, thank you! And I am speaking the Taíno language with you.
Daká “I am,” teketa “very,” xali “happy,” hahõ “thank you.” D'ahiyabó “I am speaking,” ahiyaniwa taino “Taíno language,” b'oma “with you.”

Cooking, food, and hospitality

Hamuwei buká? D'abokabó kasabe kaburẽkowa.
Are you hungry? I am cooking cassava bread on the griddle.
Hamuwei “hungry,” buká “you are.” D'abokabó “I am cooking,” kasabe “cassava bread,” kaburẽkowa “on the griddle.”
W'eikahá batata, hime, kena semiku wayaba dabohiy'oloko.
We will eat sweet potato, fish, and sweet guava inside my house.
W'eikahá “we will eat,” batata “sweet potato,” hime “fish,” kena “and,” semiku wayaba “sweet guava,” dabohiy'oloko “inside my house.”
Ikiwa, b'ataté kurula ni kahiwer'oriya!
Please, drink cold water from the gourd!
Ikiwa “please,” b'ataté “drink,” kurula ni “cold water,” kahiwer'oriya “from the gourd.”

Nature, travel, and village life

Harukarã, w'okonahá karabukoyo d'awõmoma.
Tomorrow, we will travel toward the forest with my dog.
Harukarã “tomorrow,” w'okonahá “we will travel,” karabukoyo “toward the forest,” d'awõmoma “with my dog.”
N'arikẽbá ababe hikoteya kena aba manati kabeyabukowa.
They saw some sea turtles and a manatee at the beach.
N'arikẽbá “they saw,” ababe hikoteya “some sea turtles,” kena “and,” aba manati “a manatee,” kabeyabukowa “at the beach.”
T'areiká bi sũsũ kawayabakowa, kena toká teketa anako.
The small hummingbird sings at the guava tree, and it is very beautiful.
T'areiká “it sings,” bi sũsũ “small hummingbird,” kawayabakowa “at the guava tree,” kena “and,” toká “it is,” teketa “very,” anako “beautiful.”

Storytelling, music, and evening gatherings

At night, the chief sits on his ceremonial stool at the plaza.
Kakaraya “at night,” l'aikalaká “he sits,” li kasike “the chief,” liduhokowa “on his stool,” kabateikowa “at the plaza.”
Hukãmaté to hebe urakei w'arokot'oriya!
