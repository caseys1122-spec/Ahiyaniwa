Ahiyaniwa

“Language.” A modern, carefully reconstructed Taíno language rooted in Classical sources, Arawakan comparison, and the living rhythm of the Caribbean.

Overview

A modern Taíno language

Ahiyaniwa is a modern revitalization of the Taíno language of the Greater Antilles. It is being created by Casey Alberto Roche, a Cuban-American linguist with distant Taíno ancestry. The project is still in its early stages, but work continues every day.

The name

Ahiyaniwa means “language,” built from ahiya “to speak” plus the nominalizing material -niwa. The name keeps the language centered on speech, community, and everyday use.

Arawakan roots

Taíno is a Ta-Arawakan language. Ahiyaniwa follows Arawakan patterns: pronominal prefixes, agglutinative verbs, postpositions, stative predication, and an open-syllable aesthetic.

Authenticity first

There are a few Taíno reconstructions in circulation. Ahiyaniwa strives to maintain Arawakan authenticity by working from colonial sources, historical linguistics, and close sister languages such as Lokono and Wayuu.

Project status

Ahiyaniwa is in its early stages. The dictionary is already substantial, the grammar is being standardized, and the lexicon is expanding. It is a work in progress — and it is worked on every day.

Orthography standard

This site uses a consistent modern spelling: accent marks on verb suffixes, tildes for nasal vowels, and an apostrophe between a prefix and a vowel-initial root.

Mission

Reconnecting a Caribbean language

For a long time, conventional wisdom said Taíno was completely gone, leaving behind only loanwords such as hamaka “hammock,” hurakã “hurricane,” kanowa “canoe,” and tabako “tobacco.” Ahiyaniwa refuses to let the language stay a footnote in history.

Why this project exists

For Casey Alberto Roche, a Cuban-American linguist with distant Taíno ancestry, this work is deeply personal. Reconstructing the linguistic roots of the Caribbean is not a dry academic exercise; it is a way of reconnecting with heritage. That connection (and disappointment with existing Taíno reconstructions) is what sparked the creation of Ahiyaniwa.

Ahiyaniwa is still in its early stages, but it is not static. Work continues every day: refining the lexicon, testing grammatical patterns, standardizing spelling, and expanding the available materials.

Arawakan authenticity

A few Taíno reconstructions exist, but Ahiyaniwa is built to stay close to Arawakan structure. It does not invent forms out of thin air. It follows the linguistic breadcrumbs left by 16th-century sources, historical linguists, and sister languages.

The goal is not merely to make “Taíno-sounding” words. The goal is a usable language with authentic Arawakan morphology, syntax, and phonological shape.

From colonial sources

Chroniclers such as Fray Ramón Pané, Bartolomé de las Casas, and Gonzalo Fernández de Oviedo recorded Taíno words, place names, personal names, and short phrases. But they were not trained phoneticians. They used 16th-century Spanish spelling, which is why historical spellings like Buren, Caiman, and Tiburon must be filtered into forms like burẽ, kaimã, and tiburõ.

From sister languages

When the historical record runs out, Ahiyaniwa compares Taíno with Lokono, Wayuu, and reconstructed Proto-Lokono-Wayuu roots. Regular sound correspondences help bridge lexical gaps while respecting Taíno’s open-syllable rhythm, intervocalic lenition, and Arawakan grammatical skeleton.

Mission statement: Ahiyaniwa is a labor of love: an early-stage, expanding, community-facing reconstruction that honors the original speakers by keeping the language structurally Arawakan, Caribbean in sound, and usable in the modern world.

Syllable structure

CV, V, and CVC are possible. Any consonant can occur syllable-initially, but the coda is highly restricted and consonant clusters are avoided. The language strongly favors open syllables and a smooth, vowel-final rhythm.

Template: CV, V, CVC.

Phonological rules

Allophony: /d/ → [ɾ] between vowels. In Taíno writing, d occurs word-initially, while r is used between vowels.

Stress: usually penultimate, but final if the word ends in /i/, the diphthong /ei/, or a nasal vowel. Vowel suffixes also make stress word-final.

Grammar

Arawakan grammar, clearly organized

Ahiyaniwa is agglutinative and synthetic. It uses bound morphemes for person, possession, tense, and aspect, and it prefers verb-first word order.

Pronominal prefixes

PersonSingularPlural
1stda- (my / I)wa- (our / we)
2ndbu- (your / you)hu- (your PL / you PL)
3rd Masc.li- (his / he)na- (their / they)
3rd Fem.tu- (her / she)na- (their / they)
3rd Inan.to- (its / it)na- (their / they)

Prefixes attach to nouns for possession and to verbs for the subject. Vowel-initial roots trigger elision: da- + ahiya → d’ahiya.

Independent emphatic pronouns

Formed with the focus element -kiya: dakiya “I / me / myself,” bukiya “you / yourself,” likiya “he / himself,” tukiya “she / herself,” wakiya “we / ourselves,” hukiya “you all / yourselves,” nakiya “they / themselves.” The inanimate pronoun remains to.

These are used for emphasis, contrast, or as standalone clause actors.

Object pronouns

Object pronouns are enclitics placed after the verb or attached to postpositions: de “me,” wo “us,” bu “you,” hu “you all,” li “him,” tu “her,” to “it,” nu “them.”

Articles & plurals

ka- is an attributive definite prefix for inanimate nouns. Free li marks masculine human definiteness; to marks feminine/inanimate definiteness. Indefinite aba means “a/an”; ababe / abano mean “some/several.”

Plural suffixes: -no animate/human, -be inanimate/general.

Postpositions

Relations come after the noun: -mũ “to/for,” -oriya “from/of,” -oma “with/in the company of,” -abo “using/with,” -yo “toward,” -kowa “at/on,” -oloko “inside.”

Verb template

Pronoun prefix + verb root + tense/aspect suffix. The main suffixes are -ká present/stative/habitual, -bá past/perfective, -bó progressive, and -há future/irrealis. Imperatives use -té.

The copula “to be”

The stative suffix -ká attaches directly to the pronominal root: daká “I am,” buká “you are,” liká “he is,” tuká “she is,” toká “it is,” waká “we are,” huká “you all are,” naká “they are.” Past: dabá “I was.” Future: dahá “I will be.”

Stative predication

Synthetic: pronoun prefix + adjective + -ká, e.g. daxaliká “I am happy.” Analytic: independent copula + adjective, e.g. daká xali “I am happy.” Both are valid in modern Ahiyaniwa.

Negation & commands

Negation uses ma-: matoxaliká “it is not happy.” Imperatives prefix the subject and suffix -té: b’ahiyaté! “Speak!” hukãmaté! “You all listen!”

Word order

Ahiyaniwa prefers verb-first order: V-S-O / V-S-Location. Even when a full noun subject appears, the verb keeps its matching pronoun prefix. Modifiers generally precede the noun.

Original Sentences

Miyari kena maburika! Toká d’iri Maria, tuwei toká b’iri?
Hello and welcome! My name is Maria; what is your name?
Miyari “hello” + kena “and” + maburika “welcome.” Toká “it is” + d’iri “my name.” Tuwei “what” + toká “it is” + b’iri “your name.”
Daká teketa xali, hahõ! Kena d’ahiyabó ahiyaniwa taino b’oma.
I am very happy, thank you! And I am speaking the Taíno language with you.
Daká “I am” + teketa “very” + xali “happy.” D’ahiyabó “I am speaking” + ahiyaniwa taino “Taíno language” + b’oma “with you.”
Hamuwei buká? D’abokabó kasabe kaburēkowa.
Are you hungry? I am cooking cassava bread on the griddle.
Hamuwei “hungry” + buká “you are.” D’abokabó “I am cooking” + kasabe “cassava bread” + ka-burē-kowa “on the griddle.”
Harukarã, w’okonahá karabukoyo d’awõmoma.
Tomorrow, we will travel toward the forest with my dog.
Harukarã “tomorrow” + w’okonahá “we will travel” + ka-arabuko-yo “toward the forest” + da-awõ-m-oma “with my dog.”
